[curious] A friend announces their promotion. You smile, say congratulations, and feel a small twist in your stomach. Later, you're picking holes in their success. Maybe they just got lucky. Why are you doing that? Carl Jung called the parts of ourselves we struggle to recognize or accept our shadow. That includes envy or anger, but also confidence and creativity we've learned to keep out of sight. Integration means acknowledging those parts and taking responsibility for their expression. A feeling doesn't have to become an action. Here are seven practical ways to begin, inspired by Jung's ideas. Start with whichever feels familiar.

One. Notice the moments that stay with you. The ones you replay long after they've happened. Someone interrupts you in a meeting. You're still arguing with them in your head while washing dishes. Pause. What actually happened? What did you feel? What did you want to do? Maybe you felt dismissed. You wanted to interrupt back, leave, or prove you were the smartest person there. Write it down plainly. A few honest sentences give you more to work with than a sweeping judgment. You don't need to explain your entire personality. Get curious about this reaction, in this situation.

Separate the event from your interpretation. They interrupted me is an observation you can examine. Nobody respects me is a bigger conclusion. It may feel true without describing everything that happened. Look for repetition. Does that feeling return when you're corrected, overlooked, or asked for help? Strong reactions have many explanations, including tiredness or genuine mistreatment. They aren't proof of a hidden flaw. Still, a pattern raises a useful question: what feels threatened here, and why does it matter? Leave room for an answer you haven't considered. You can investigate without putting yourself on trial.

Two. Question your harshest judgments. Jung described projection as seeing in others qualities we don't acknowledge in ourselves. Every annoying person isn't secretly a mirror. Sometimes their behavior really is inconsiderate. Still, why does one particular trait get so much of your attention? It's worth exploring. A colleague mentions their achievements. You think, I can't stand people who need attention. Sound familiar? Perhaps they're dominating the conversation. Or perhaps you've made it impossible to acknowledge anything you're proud of. Both are possible. You can ask for a fairer conversation while examining your own rules.

Complete this sentence: I judge people who do this because I believe it means they're... Then ask: do I recognize any of that impulse in myself? Be specific. Don't force an answer. Maybe you want credit but wait for people to notice. Then you resent them when they don't. Here's a possibility: wanting recognition doesn't automatically make me arrogant. I can ask for it honestly. Next time, describe your contribution without apologizing for it. Make space for yourself and others. Stay open to evidence. This exercise should help you see people more clearly, including yourself.

[gently] Three. Write the sentence you avoid. Find five quiet minutes with a notebook, somewhere you feel comfortable. Start with, what I don't want to admit is. Finish without trying to sound wise. I wanted them to fail. I'm tired of helping. I wish someone would take care of me. You may dislike what you've written. Leave it there a moment, without making it a verdict. Noticing a spiteful thought doesn't mean spite is your whole identity. Keep that difference in view. You're describing your experience so you can understand it. Nobody else needs to read this draft.

Add another sentence: what I wanted in that moment was. Does anything more specific come into focus? Behind wishing someone would fail, you might find disappointment about a project you haven't dared to start. Behind being tired of helping, you might find you've agreed to more than you can carry. These are possibilities, not hidden meanings you have to discover. An explanation should fit your experience. End with one reasonable next step. Ask for help, make time, or acknowledge your disappointment. Then close the notebook. You don't have to spend the evening taking yourself apart.

Four. Loosen the identity you defend. What sentence describes the kind of person you are? I'm easygoing. I'm always independent. Everyone can rely on me. Each may describe something real. Trouble starts when you protect that description at the expense of what you're actually feeling. If you're always easygoing, where does frustration go? If you're independent, can you ask for support? The label is like an outgrown jacket. Appreciate what it gave you, and loosen it. Add two words: sometimes, and also. I'm sometimes patient. I also get frustrated and need breaks.

That sounds small. But it gives you a more honest sentence for an ordinary conversation. Instead of, whatever you want, try, I'd prefer somewhere quiet tonight. No dramatic announcement needed. Instead of accepting another task, try, I can help tomorrow, but I don't have time today. People may be surprised if you rarely express preferences. Give them a chance to hear you. Allow yourself to feel awkward, too. An unfamiliar response doesn't have to be a dishonest one. You can be kind, capable, and dependable while being accurate about your limits. There's room for both.

Five. Give the feeling a responsible outlet. Acknowledging anger doesn't require saying every angry thought aloud. Someone keeps taking credit for your work. Your anger may point to something worth addressing directly. Wait until you can speak clearly. Describe what happened and what you'd like to change. Try, I prepared that section. Next meeting, please make my contribution clear when you present it. You haven't buried your anger or used it to humiliate someone. You've chosen a response. Ask the same of other feelings: what response can I stand behind afterward?

Envy might prompt you to practice. Wanting attention might prompt you to share work you've hidden. Feelings don't guarantee good advice. Consider the facts, your values, and the effects on others. Too worked up to do that? Leave the message unsent. Read it later, more carefully. Already lashed out? Take responsibility for what you did. Explaining it doesn't erase its impact. I was embarrassed, and I spoke unfairly. I'm sorry. Here's what I'll do differently next time. That's practical integration: notice an impulse, consider it honestly, and practice a response you'd like to repeat.

Six. Reclaim a quality you admire. Jung's shadow also includes valuable qualities we haven't acknowledged or developed. Whose confidence, playfulness, or creativity catches your attention? What do they give themselves permission to do? Maybe they ask questions, make imperfect art, or rest without explaining why they've earned it. Admiration doesn't prove equal ability. But it can reveal something you'd like to practice yourself. What makes that quality feel unavailable? Have you learned that being visible means showing off? Or that playing wastes time? Name the rule, then decide whether you still agree.

Try a small version. Ask one question, draw one page, or take an unhurried break. You don't need to become that person. You're allowing more range in your own behavior. If creativity was dismissed growing up, making something may feel embarrassing before it's enjoyable. Go slowly. Notice what happens. Did someone criticize you, or were you anticipating criticism that never arrived? If it goes badly, adjust. One uncomfortable afternoon doesn't settle what you're allowed to enjoy. Keep practicing that willingness to want things, learn things, and let yourself be seen trying.

Seven. Let someone help you see. We can't notice everything when we're the only person asking questions. Choose someone honest and considerate. Ask about a specific pattern, rather than your entire character. Try, when I feel criticized, do I interrupt before you've finished? What have you noticed? Ask for an example. You don't have to agree immediately. Their interpretation isn't automatically the truth. But if it matches something you've noticed, consider it before rushing to explain it away. You might say, I can see that. Next time, I'll try letting you finish before responding.

[reflective] If reflection becomes overwhelming, pause. A qualified therapist can help you explore it with support. For now, choose one exercise this week. Notice a reaction, write honestly, or try a small change. You don't need complete self-understanding to behave differently. The next ordinary moment gives you somewhere to begin. Remember your friend's promotion? You might still feel envy. This time, you can admit it's there. Congratulate them sincerely. Then consider what you want to work toward. You can be happy for them and want more yourself. That's a practical beginning: recognizing more of yourself, taking responsibility, and choosing what to do next with your eyes open.
