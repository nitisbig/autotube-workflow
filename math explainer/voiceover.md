Newton’s second law is written as:

F equals m times a.

Here, F is the net force acting on an object, m is the object’s mass, and a is its acceleration.

The equation tells us something simple but powerful:

Force causes acceleration.

Suppose we have a two-kilogram box, and we push it with a net force of ten newtons.

Using F equals m a, we can solve for acceleration.

Divide both sides by mass.

So, a equals F divided by m.

Ten newtons divided by two kilograms gives an acceleration of five meters per second squared.

This also tells us how force and mass affect motion.

If we increase the force while keeping the mass the same, the acceleration increases.

Double the force, and you double the acceleration.

But if we increase the mass while keeping the force the same, the acceleration decreases.

Double the mass, and the acceleration becomes half as large.

So F equals m a connects three things:

force, mass, and acceleration.

More force means more acceleration.

More mass means less acceleration for the same force.

And one important detail: F means the net force — the total force after all forces acting on the object are combined.

That is Newton’s second law:

the acceleration of an object depends on how much net force acts on it, and how much mass it has.
